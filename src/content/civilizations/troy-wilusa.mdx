## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Troy — the city of Priam and Hector, besieged by Agamemnon's Greeks in Homer's *Iliad* — has fascinated humanity for millennia. The archaeological site at **Hisarlik** in northwestern Turkey, first excavated by Heinrich Schliemann in the 1870s, reveals a layered city occupied for over 3,000 years. Modern scholarship identifies it with **Wilusa**, a city known from Hittite texts, placing the legendary city firmly in the network of Late Bronze Age Anatolian powers.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Archaeological Layers

<Cited status="green">
Hisarlik contains at least nine major occupation layers (Troy I–IX):
- **Troy I–V** (c. 3000–1750 BCE) — Early Bronze Age settlements; Troy II contained Schliemann's famous "Treasure of Priam" (actually ~1,000 years too early for the Trojan War)
- **Troy VI** (c. 1750–1300 BCE) — A prosperous fortified citadel with impressive walls; destroyed possibly by earthquake
- **Troy VIIa** (c. 1300–1180 BCE) — The most likely candidate for the "Homeric Troy," showing signs of destruction by fire and possible siege. Manfred Korfmann's excavations (1988–2005) revealed a substantial lower city, making the site much larger than previously thought
- **Troy VIII–IX** — Hellenistic and Roman Ilion, visited by Alexander the Great and celebrated by Augustus<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Hittite Connection: Wilusa

<Cited status="yellow">
Hittite cuneiform texts mention **Wilusa**, a vassal state in northwestern Anatolia. The **Alaksandu Treaty** (c. 1280 BCE) between the Hittite king Muwatalli II and King Alaksandu of Wilusa is striking — "Alaksandu" closely resembles "Alexandros," the alternate name of Paris of Troy in Homer. The Hittite texts also reference **Ahhiyawa**, which many scholars identify with Mycenaean Greece (Homer's Achaeans).
</Cited>

## The Trojan War Question

<Cited status="yellow">
Did the Trojan War happen? The scholarly consensus is nuanced: while Homer's *Iliad* is not a historical document, the archaeological evidence at Troy VIIa is consistent with violent destruction around 1180 BCE, and the Hittite texts confirm both the geographic and political setting. A historical kernel — perhaps a Mycenaean raid or siege — likely underlies the epic tradition.
</Cited>

## The Alaksandu Treaty and the 'Alexandros' Question

<Cited status="green">
The Hittite cuneiform tablet **CTH 76** (KUB 21.1), discovered at Hattusa, records a vassal treaty between Muwatalli II and a king named **Alaksandu of Wilusa**, dated c. 1280 BCE. Three details make the document remarkable as evidence for the Trojan-cycle background: the city name *Wilusa* is phonologically equivalent to *(W)ilios* (the older form of *Ilios* preserved by the digamma in Homer's metrical text); the royal name *Alaksandu* is a transparent rendering of Greek *Alexandros*, the alternative name of Paris of Troy; and the treaty preamble names **Appaliunaš** among the divine witnesses — possibly an early form of the god *Apollon*, who in the *Iliad* is Troy's chief divine protector.<Ref id={6} /><Ref id={3} /> None of this proves the historicity of the Trojan War, but it firmly anchors Wilusa-Ilios in the Late Bronze Age political and religious imagination.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Korfmann's Lower City

<Cited status="green">
Manfred Korfmann's renewed German–American excavations at Hisarlik (1988–2005) used magnetometry to detect a fortification ditch enclosing a **Lower City** of roughly 30 hectares south and east of the citadel — at least ten times the area Schliemann and Blegen had identified. The lower city contains hundreds of pithos burials, drainage works, and evidence of mixed Anatolian-Mycenaean material culture.<Ref id={7} /> The find supports Korfmann's claim, controversial at the time but now widely accepted, that Late Bronze Age Troy VI / VIIa was a *Königsburg* commensurate with other Anatolian capitals — not the small chieftain's hill that minimalist readings of Homer had assumed.<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>
