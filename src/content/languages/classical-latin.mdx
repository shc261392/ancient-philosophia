## Overview

<Cited status="yellow">
Classical Latin is the standardized literary form of the Latin language used roughly from the late Roman Republic through the early Roman Empire (c. 75 BCE–200 CE). It is the language of Cicero's orations, Caesar's war commentaries, Virgil's *Aeneid*, Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, Horace's odes, and Tacitus's histories. Latin went on to serve as the language of scholarship, the Catholic Church, science, and law for over a millennium after Rome's fall, and is the parent of the Romance languages (French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian).<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## The Latin Alphabet

<Cited status="yellow">
The Latin alphabet, derived from the Greek alphabet via the Etruscan script, had 23 letters in the Classical period (J, U, and W were added later):<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
A B C D E F G H I K L M N O P Q R S T V X Y Z<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
In Classical Latin: **V** represented both [u] and [w], **I** could be both [i] and [j], and **C** was always [k].<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Grammar Highlights

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Nouns** — Five declensions, three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), six cases (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, vocative) — the ablative case is distinctively Latin
- **Verbs** — Four conjugations; six tenses (present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect, future perfect); three moods (indicative, subjunctive, imperative); active and passive voices
- **The Ablative Absolute** — A distinctively Latin construction (participial phrase in the ablative case functioning as an adverbial clause)
- **Indirect Statement** — Accusative + infinitive construction (unlike Greek, which uses ὅτι + finite verb)
- **Word order** — Typically SOV (Subject-Object-Verb), but flexible due to the case system; verb often at the end of its clause<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Key Texts for Students

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Caesar, *De Bello Gallico*** — Clear military prose; traditionally the first unadapted text students read
- **Cicero, orations** (*In Catilinam*, *Pro Caelio*) — The gold standard of Latin prose
- **Virgil, *Aeneid*** — Rome's national epic; the pinnacle of Latin poetry
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses*** — Mythological epic in elegant, readable hexameters
- **Catullus** — Short, vivid poems; accessible for intermediate students
- **Horace, *Odes*** and ***Satires*** — Lyric poetry and witty social commentary
- **Tacitus, *Annals*** — Brilliant but challenging historical prose
</Cited>

## Recommended Learning Path

<Cited status="yellow">
1. **Learn morphology systematically** (noun declensions, verb conjugations)
2. **Work through a textbook** (Wheelock's, LLPSI, or Shelmerdine — see below)
3. **Read adapted texts** (LLPSI's *Familia Romana* provides extensive comprehensible input)
4. **Progress to Caesar** (the traditional bridge to unadapted Latin)
5. **Read Cicero and Virgil** (with commentary)
6. **Expand to poetry, historiography, philosophy**
</Cited>

## Sample Text

<Cited status="green">
Opening of Caesar's *De Bello Gallico* (Book I.1), edited in the Oxford Classical Text by Renatus du Pontet:<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="green">
> *Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres, quarum unam incolunt Belgae, aliam Aquitani, tertiam qui ipsorum lingua Celtae, nostra Galli appellantur.*
>
> "All Gaul is divided into three parts, one of which the Belgae inhabit, another the Aquitani, the third those who in their own language are called Celts, in ours Gauls."<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
This is perhaps the most famous opening line in Latin literature, and Caesar's clear military prose makes it a traditional starting point for students reading unadapted Latin.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Pronunciation and the Reconstructed Classical Sound

<Cited status="green">
The pronunciation of Classical Latin reconstructed by W. Sidney Allen's *Vox Latina* — the scholarly standard — differs systematically from both ecclesiastical and traditional school pronunciations: *C* is always /k/, *V* is /w/ in consonant position and /u/ in vowel position, *AE* is the diphthong /ai̯/, and vowel length is phonemic.<Ref id={10} /> Internal evidence comes from poetic metre (Virgil and Horace presuppose vowel-length distinctions), Greek transliterations of Latin words, and the comments of grammarians such as Quintilian.<Ref id={11} />
</Cited>

## Reconstructed Pronunciation

<Cited status="green">
The **Classical pronunciation** (*pronuntiatio restituta*) reconstructed by W. Sidney Allen's *Vox Latina* is now the consensus standard in universities worldwide. Key features established by grammarians like Quintilian, by Greek transliterations of Latin words, and by Romance comparative reconstruction include: *c* and *g* always hard (Cicero = /ˈkɪkɛroː/, not /ˈsɪsɛroʊ/); *v* pronounced as English /w/ (veni = /ˈweːniː/); long vowels phonemically distinct from short (*malum* "evil" vs *mālum* "apple"); *ae* as a diphthong /ai̯/, not /eː/; and a true geminate contrast for double consonants (*annus* /ˈannus/ vs *anus* /ˈanus/).<Ref id={4} /> The medieval "ecclesiastical" pronunciation (used in liturgy and in some 19th-century textbooks) reflects Italian phonological developments rather than ancient usage.<Ref id={6} />
</Cited>
