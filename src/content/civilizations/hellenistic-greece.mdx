## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The **Hellenistic period** began with Alexander the Great's death in **323 BCE** and ended, by convention, with the Roman conquest of Greece (**146 BCE**, destruction of Corinth) or the death of Cleopatra VII and the annexation of Egypt (**30 BCE**). It was an era in which Greek language and culture spread from the Mediterranean to Central Asia, producing a cosmopolitan civilization that fused Greek, Egyptian, Persian, and Mesopotamian traditions.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="green">
The word "Hellenistic" (Ἑλληνιστικός) was coined by the 19th-century historian Johann Gustav Droysen to describe this world of cultural mixing, distinguishing it from the "Hellenic" (purely Greek) civilization of the Classical period.<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

## The Wars of the Diadochi (323–281 BCE)

<Cited status="yellow">
Alexander died in Babylon in June 323 BCE leaving no viable heir. His generals — the **Diadochi** (Διάδοχοι, "Successors") — fought five devastating wars over his empire, eventually splitting it into rival kingdoms:<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Ptolemaic Egypt** — Founded by **Ptolemy I Soter**, controlling Egypt, Cyrenaica, and Cyprus
- **Seleucid Empire** — Founded by **Seleucus I Nicator**, controlling Syria, Mesopotamia, Iran, and at times Anatolia
- **Antigonid Macedonia** — Founded by descendants of **Antigonus I Monophthalmus**, controlling Macedonia and (nominally) Greece
- **Attalid Pergamum** — A later breakaway kingdom in western Anatolia, founded by the Attalid dynasty<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## The Hellenistic World

### Alexandria

<Cited status="green">
**Alexandria**, founded by Alexander in 331 BCE, became the cultural capital of the Hellenistic world under the Ptolemies. Its two defining institutions shaped intellectual history:<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
- **The Mouseion** (Museum, "Temple of the Muses") — a state-funded research institution where scholars resided and worked under royal patronage; it has been called the first university in any meaningful sense
- **The Library of Alexandria** — the ancient world's greatest collection of texts, reportedly holding 400,000–700,000 scroll-volumes at its peak. The Ptolemies pursued acquisitions aggressively, allegedly confiscating texts from ships in Alexandria's harbor and returning copies in their place<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
Scholars working at the Mouseion included **Euclid** (geometry, *Elements*), **Eratosthenes** (who calculated the Earth's circumference to remarkable accuracy by measuring shadows at Syene and Alexandria), **Aristarchus of Samos** (who proposed a heliocentric model of the solar system), and **Callimachus** (poet, scholar, and compiler of the *Pinakes* catalog of the Library).<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

### Science and Technology

<Cited status="green">
The Hellenistic period was the golden age of ancient science:<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Archimedes of Syracuse** (c. 287–212 BCE) — mathematics, mechanics, hydrostatics; the Archimedean screw, the law of buoyancy, and on-the-fly war machines defending Syracuse
- **Hipparchus of Nicaea** (c. 190–120 BCE) — trigonometry, the first systematic star catalog, the discovery of the precession of the equinoxes
- **Hero of Alexandria** (c. 10–70 CE) — the *aeolipile* (first known steam engine), automated theatrical devices, and the *Mechanica*
- **Ctesibius of Alexandria** — hydraulics, the water clock (*clepsydra*), the pneumatic organ (*hydraulis*)<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

### Philosophy

<Cited status="yellow">
The Hellenistic age produced three major new philosophical schools at Athens, each addressing the question of how the individual should live in a world where the old *polis* no longer provided a stable moral framework:<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Stoicism** (Zeno of Citium, c. 300 BCE) — the cosmos is rational and ordered by *logos*; virtue is living in accordance with nature; external circumstances (wealth, health, reputation) are morally "indifferent"
- **Epicureanism** (Epicurus, c. 307 BCE) — the gods exist but do not intervene in human affairs; the universe is composed of atoms moving in the void; *ataraxia* (freedom from disturbance) is the highest good
- **Skepticism** (Pyrrho of Elis, Carneades of the New Academy) — certain knowledge is impossible; the suspension of judgment (*epochē*) leads to tranquility<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
All three schools responded to the same problem: in a world without the stable city-state (*polis*) of the Classical period, how should an individual live?
</Cited>

### Koine Greek

<Cited status="yellow">
The Hellenistic period saw the emergence of **Koine Greek** (ἡ κοινὴ διάλεκτος, "the common dialect") as the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean — replacing the classical Attic, Ionic, Doric dialects with a simplified, widely-understood standard. Koine was the language of commerce, administration, the New Testament, and the Septuagint (Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible).
</Cited>

## Cultural Fusion

<Cited status="yellow">
Hellenistic culture was defined by **syncretism** — the merging of Greek and non-Greek traditions across the territories Alexander's successors ruled:<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Greco-Egyptian religion** — the new cult of Serapis instituted by Ptolemy I to unite Greek and Egyptian subjects; the hellenized worship of Isis, which spread across the Mediterranean
- **Greco-Buddhist art** — in Gandhara (modern Pakistan/Afghanistan), the Buddha was for the first time depicted in human form, using Greek sculptural conventions of drapery and contrapposto inherited from Bactrian Greek workshops
- **Hellenistic Judaism** — Jewish writers composed in Greek (Philo of Alexandria, Josephus); the Septuagint translation of the Hebrew Bible made Jewish scripture available to the Greek-speaking world<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

## Decline and Roman Conquest

<Cited status="yellow">
The Hellenistic kingdoms were gradually absorbed by Rome:
- **Macedonia** fell after the Battle of Pydna (**168 BCE**) and became a Roman province in 146 BCE
- **Greece** was sacked after the destruction of Corinth (**146 BCE**)
- **Pergamum** was bequeathed to Rome by Attalus III (**133 BCE**)
- **Seleucid Empire** was reduced by Pompey (**63 BCE**)
- **Ptolemaic Egypt** fell with Cleopatra VII (**30 BCE**)
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
But Hellenistic culture outlived Hellenistic politics. Greek remained the lingua franca of the eastern Roman Empire for centuries. Roman art, philosophy, religion, and science were profoundly shaped by Hellenistic models. The common saying was: "Captured Greece took her fierce captor captive" (Horace, *Epistles* 2.1.156).
</Cited>
