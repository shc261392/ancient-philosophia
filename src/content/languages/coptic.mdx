## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Coptic is the latest stage of the ancient Egyptian language, written in the **Greek alphabet** supplemented by six or seven letters derived from Demotic script for sounds not found in Greek. Unlike earlier Egyptian scripts (hieroglyphic, hieratic, demotic), Coptic **writes vowels**, making it invaluable for reconstructing the pronunciation of earlier Egyptian. Coptic was the everyday language of Egypt until Arabic replaced it after the Arab conquest (641 CE), though it survived as a spoken language in some communities until perhaps the 17th century. It remains the **liturgical language** of the Coptic Orthodox Church.<Ref id={4} />
</Cited>

## Dialects

<Cited status="green">
- **Sahidic** — The standard literary dialect of Upper Egypt; most Coptic texts are in Sahidic
- **Bohairic** — The dialect of Lower Egypt (the Delta); the current liturgical standard
- **Fayyumic, Akhmimic, Lycopolitan** — Regional dialects with smaller text corpora<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

## Key Texts

<Cited status="green">
- **Nag Hammadi Library** — 4th-century collection of Gnostic texts in Coptic (including the Gospel of Thomas)
- **Coptic Bible translations** — Among the earliest Bible translations
- **Shenoute of Atripe** — The greatest Coptic literary author (4th–5th century)
- **Apophthegmata Patrum** — Sayings of the Desert Fathers<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="green">
The Nag Hammadi codices, discovered in December 1945 near the Upper-Egyptian village of Hamra Dum and now in the Coptic Museum in Cairo, contain thirteen 4th-century books in Sahidic Coptic preserving fifty-two distinct texts, including the *Gospel of Thomas*, the *Gospel of Philip*, and the *Apocryphon of John*.<Ref id={9} /> James Robinson's 1977 *Nag Hammadi Library in English* and Marvin Meyer's 2007 *Nag Hammadi Scriptures* remain the standard English translations.<Ref id={10} />
</Cited>

## Sahidic, Bohairic, and the Coptic Dialects

<Cited status="green">
Coptic was never monolithic. Layton's *Coptic Grammar* and Funk's dialect studies distinguish at least six major literary dialects: **Sahidic** (the classical literary standard from c. 350 CE onward, originally based on Upper Egyptian speech around Thebes), **Bohairic** (the Delta dialect, which became the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church and replaced Sahidic in the medieval period), **Akhmimic**, **Lycopolitan / Subakhmimic**, **Fayyumic**, and **Oxyrhynchite** (also called Mesokemic).<Ref id={2} /> Most modern textbooks teach Sahidic because the Nag Hammadi codices, the bulk of pre-medieval monastic literature (Shenoute, Pachomius), and the earliest complete Coptic New Testament are all Sahidic; Bohairic is essential for anyone working on Coptic Orthodox liturgy, and the minor dialects are mostly important for textual criticism of the Coptic Old Testament translations.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>
