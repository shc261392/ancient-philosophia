## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Sumerian is the earliest known written language, attested from around 3400 BCE in southern Mesopotamia. It is a **language isolate** with no known relatives. Even after it ceased to be a spoken vernacular (by roughly 2000 BCE), Sumerian continued as a scholarly, literary, and liturgical language for another two millennia — comparable to the role of Latin in medieval Europe.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Key Features

<Cited status="green">
- **Agglutinative** — Words are built by chaining morphemes together (unlike the fusional structure of Semitic or Indo-European languages)
- **Ergative-absolutive** — The subject of a transitive verb is marked differently from the subject of an intransitive verb
- **Verb chain** — The Sumerian verbal chain can encode subject, object, indirect object, and various other elements in a single complex form
- **Emesal** — A special dialect used in literature by female characters and in certain liturgical contexts<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
Enheduanna (c. 2285–2250 BCE), daughter of Sargon of Akkad and *en*-priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur, is the earliest historically attested named author in world history; her authorship of *nin-me-šar₂-ra* and other hymns is debated but defended by most current scholarship.<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

## Sumerian as a Scholarly Language

<Cited status="green">
By the end of the Ur III period (c. 2000 BCE), Sumerian had ceased to be a vernacular and was replaced by Akkadian in everyday use. But the Old Babylonian scribal schools (*e₂-dub-ba-a*) of the early 2nd millennium BCE deliberately preserved and copied Sumerian literary classics, producing the bulk of the literary corpus we possess today — hymns, debate poems, royal praise, and the Sumerian Gilgamesh cycle.<Ref id={10} /> Sumerian remained the prestige liturgical and scholarly language of Mesopotamia down to the Seleucid period (1st century BCE), used in cultic recitation, omen literature, and the curriculum of the *šatammu*-priests of Babylon and Uruk — a span of over 2,000 years after it stopped being spoken.<Ref id={11} />
</Cited>
