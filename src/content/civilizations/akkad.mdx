## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The Akkadian Empire, founded by Sargon of Akkad (r. c. 2334–2279 BCE), was the first known empire in world history. Sargon, whose name means "the true king," rose from obscure origins — legend says he was a cupbearer — to conquer all of Sumer and extend his rule from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean. The empire unified Sumerian and Akkadian (Semitic) speaking populations under a centralized administration, establishing a model of imperial governance that influenced all subsequent Near Eastern empires.<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

## Sargon and His Dynasty

<Cited status="yellow">
Sargon's rise was dramatic: he overthrew Lugalzagesi of Uruk and then conquered city after city. His daughter **Enheduanna** became the high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur and is the earliest author known by name — her hymns to Inanna are among the oldest signed literary works. Sargon's grandson **Naram-Sin** (r. c. 2254–2218 BCE) expanded the empire to its greatest extent and took the unprecedented title "King of the Four Quarters" and may have deified himself during his own lifetime.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Culture & Legacy

<Cited status="yellow">
The Akkadian period saw the Akkadian language begin its rise as the lingua franca of Mesopotamia — a status it would hold for over a millennium. Akkadian art introduced a new naturalism and dynamism, exemplified by the bronze head from Nineveh (possibly depicting Sargon or Naram-Sin) and Naram-Sin's Victory Stele. The fusion of Sumerian and Akkadian culture during this period produced a bilingual scholarly tradition that endured for millennia.
</Cited>

## Enheduanna and the Earliest Named Authorship

<Cited status="green">
Sargon's daughter **Enheduanna** (*En-he-du-ana*, c. 2285–2250 BCE), high priestess (*EN*) of Nanna at Ur, is the earliest individual author in world literature whose name is preserved with the works attributed to her.<Ref id={6} /> Her hymn cycle includes *Nin-me-šára* ("The Exaltation of Inanna"), a 153-line theological self-defence that re-positions the Akkadian war-goddess Inanna at the apex of the Sumerian pantheon — a literary act mirroring Sargon's political union of Sumer and Akkad.<Ref id={7} /> Sophus Helle's annotated 2023 translation makes the full Enheduanna corpus newly available to English readers.<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

## Imperial Administration and Material Culture

<Cited status="green">
The Akkadian period is also the first time we can document **standardised imperial administration**: the calendar of Akkad replaced local calendars across conquered cities; royal year-names commemorated specific events; weights and measures were normalised.<Ref id={9} /> The Victory Stele of Naram-Sin, looted from Sippar to Susa by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte in the 12th century BCE and now in the Louvre, breaks Mesopotamian convention by depicting the king at near-divine scale, ascending a mountain, the divine star floating above him — the first surviving royal monument to overtly assert the king's divinity.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Collapse

<Cited status="yellow">
The empire collapsed around 2154 BCE under pressure from the **Gutians**, a mountain people from the Zagros, against a backdrop of severe regional aridification documented in marine cores from the Gulf of Oman dated to c. 2200 BCE — the so-called **4.2-kiloyear event**.<Ref id={10} /> The Sumerian literary text *The Curse of Agade* blames the fall on Naram-Sin's sacrilege against Enlil's temple at Nippur — a theological explanation typical of Mesopotamian historiography.<Ref id={3} /> The Gutian interlude was followed by the Sumerian renaissance of the Third Dynasty of Ur.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>
