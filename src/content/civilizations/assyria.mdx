## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Assyria was one of the great civilizations of ancient Mesopotamia, centered in the Upper Tigris region of northern Iraq around the cities of Ashur, Nineveh, Nimrud (Kalhu), and Dur-Sharrukin. Over more than two millennia, Assyria evolved from a small city-state into the largest empire the world had yet seen, renowned for its military prowess, administrative sophistication, and monumental art.<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

## Old Assyrian Period (c. 2000–1750 BCE)

<Cited status="green">
The earliest well-documented phase saw Assyrian merchants establish trading colonies (*kārum*) in Anatolia, most notably at Kanesh (modern Kültepe, Turkey). Thousands of cuneiform tablets from this period document a sophisticated long-distance trade network dealing in tin and textiles — providing invaluable evidence for Bronze Age commerce.<Ref id={4} />
</Cited>

## Middle Assyrian Period (c. 1400–1050 BCE)

<Cited status="yellow">
Under kings like **Ashur-uballit I** and **Tukulti-Ninurta I**, Assyria grew into a significant territorial power, rivaling Babylon, the Hittites, and Egypt. The Middle Assyrian Laws provide one of the most important legal collections from ancient Mesopotamia.<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>

## Neo-Assyrian Empire (c. 911–609 BCE)

<Cited status="yellow">
The Neo-Assyrian Empire represents Assyria's apex. Key rulers include:
- **Ashurnasirpal II** — Built the magnificent Northwest Palace at Nimrud with its famous carved reliefs
- **Tiglath-Pileser III** — Transformed Assyria into a true empire through administrative reform and mass deportation policies
- **Sargon II** — Built the new capital Dur-Sharrukin (Khorsabad) and conquered Samaria
- **Sennacherib** — Made Nineveh the imperial capital; his siege of Jerusalem is recorded in both Assyrian and biblical sources
- **Ashurbanipal** — The scholarly warrior-king who assembled the great **Library of Nineveh**, preserving thousands of cuneiform texts including the Epic of Gilgamesh
</Cited>

## Art & Architecture

<Cited status="yellow">
Assyrian palace reliefs are among the masterpieces of ancient art — vast narrative programs depicting royal hunts, military campaigns, and ritual scenes. The lion-hunt reliefs of Ashurbanipal (now in the British Museum) are widely considered the finest achievements of Mesopotamian sculpture.
</Cited>

## Fall of Assyria

<Cited status="yellow">
The **Babylonian Chronicle ABC 3** ('Fall of Nineveh Chronicle', BM 21901) records year-by-year the events of 616–609 BCE: the coalition of Nabopolassar's Babylonians and Cyaxares' Medes (the Umman-manda) besieged and sacked Nineveh in 612 BCE, and destroyed the last Assyrian holdouts at Harran by 609 BCE — among the most sudden collapses of a major power in ancient history.<Ref id={6} /><Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## The Library of Ashurbanipal

<Cited status="green">
When Hormuzd Rassam excavated the North Palace of Nineveh in 1853, he uncovered the library that **Ashurbanipal** (r. 668–c. 627 BCE) had assembled at vast expense — agents combing the temple archives of Babylonia and Borsippa for tablets to copy. The c. 30,000 fragments that survive include literary works (the Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the *Enuma Elish*, the *Erra Epic*), omen series (*Enuma Anu Enlil*, *Šumma ālu*), medical and astronomical compendia, and royal annals — a self-conscious attempt to centralise the Mesopotamian intellectual tradition in a single imperial collection.<Ref id={7} /> The British Museum's **Ashurbanipal Library Project** is currently re-cataloguing the corpus and making digital transliterations freely available.<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

## Assyrian Palace Reliefs as Imperial Propaganda

<Cited status="green">
The carved limestone wall slabs that lined Neo-Assyrian throne rooms (Ashurnasirpal II at Kalhu, Sennacherib at Nineveh, Ashurbanipal's lion hunts) constitute the most extensive surviving narrative-art programme of the ancient Near East. Julian Reade and Pauline Albenda have shown how the cycles — military campaigns, royal hunts, ritual scenes — function as carefully scripted propaganda: the king is always larger than his retinue, enemies always smaller, the conventions of perspective always serving the assertion of universal kingship.<Ref id={6} /> The corpus is concentrated in the British Museum and the Louvre; the **Khorsabad reliefs** of Sargon II form the only Assyrian narrative cycle in the United States, at the Oriental Institute in Chicago.<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>
