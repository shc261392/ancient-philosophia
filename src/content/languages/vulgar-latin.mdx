## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Vulgar Latin (*sermo vulgaris*) refers to the spoken, colloquial forms of Latin used by ordinary people across the Roman Empire, as distinct from the polished literary language of Cicero and Virgil. It is not a single dialect but a spectrum of regional and social varieties whose divergence over centuries gave rise to the **Romance languages**: French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, Catalan, Occitan, and others. Evidence for Vulgar Latin comes from graffiti, private letters, legal documents, Christian texts, and "mistakes" in formal inscriptions.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Key Differences from Classical Latin

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Case system collapse** — The six-case system reduced to two or three; prepositions replaced case functions
- **Loss of neuter gender** — Neuter nouns merged with masculine
- **Analytic verb forms** — Synthetic futures and passives replaced by periphrases: *cantare habeo* → French *chanterai* ("I will sing")
- **Vowel changes** — The 10-vowel system (5 long + 5 short) collapsed into 7 vowels based on quality rather than length
- **h-dropping** — The sound /h/ was lost early: *habere* → *avere*
- **Word order** — Shift from SOV to SVO<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## From Vulgar Latin to the Romance Languages

<Cited status="green">
The transition from spoken Latin to the early Romance vernaculars is documented in three principal text-types: the *Appendix Probi* (a 3rd–4th-century list of 227 corrections from popular to standard forms, e.g. *vetulus non veclus*), the **Reichenau Glosses** (8th-century glosses replacing Vulgate vocabulary with Old French equivalents), and the **Strasbourg Oaths** of 842 CE — the earliest surviving connected text in Old French and Old High German.<Ref id={7} /> Roger Wright's *Late Latin and Early Romance* and James Adams's *Regional Diversification of Latin* synthesise the linguistic evidence into a continuum model rather than a sudden break.<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

## Key Sources

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Pompeii graffiti** — Thousands of informal inscriptions preserved by Vesuvius (79 CE)
- **Appendix Probi** — A 3rd/4th-century grammar correction list ("say X, not Y") revealing common speech
- **Peregrinatio Aetheriae** — A 4th-century pilgrim's travel diary in popular Latin
- **Gregory of Tours, *Historia Francorum*** — 6th-century history written in Merovingian Latin
- **Curse tablets (defixiones)** — Lead tablets with colloquial language
</Cited>

## From Vulgar Latin to the Romance Languages

<Cited status="green">
The transition from Vulgar Latin to the early Romance languages is usually placed in the 7th–9th centuries, but it is the *recognition* of a divergent spoken norm — not the divergence itself — that marks the boundary. The 813 Council of Tours instructed bishops to preach "in *rusticam Romanam linguam* aut Thiotiscam" (rustic Roman tongue or Germanic), which Roger Wright reads as the first official admission that Latin sermons could no longer be understood by the unlettered.<Ref id={1} /> The Strasbourg Oaths of 842 then preserve the earliest sustained text of recognisably Old French (and Old High German). What changed phonologically is well documented by Adams and by the *Appendix Probi*: loss of phonemic vowel length, lenition of intervocalic stops, palatalisation of *c/g* before front vowels, collapse of the case system, and the rise of analytic constructions (*habeo cantatum* → *j'ai chanté*).<Ref id={2} /><Ref id={4} />
</Cited>
