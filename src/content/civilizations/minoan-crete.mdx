## Overview

<Cited status="yellow">
The Minoan civilization, named by Sir Arthur Evans after the legendary King Minos, was Europe's first advanced civilization. Centered on the island of Crete in the southern Aegean, the Minoans built elaborate palatial complexes, created vibrant frescoes, developed extensive trade networks across the Mediterranean, and employed at least two writing systems — of which **Linear A** remains undeciphered.<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>

## Palatial Complexes

<Cited status="yellow">
The great Minoan palaces were not merely royal residences but served as administrative, economic, religious, and ceremonial centers:
- **Knossos** — The largest, excavated and controversially reconstructed by Evans; contained over 1,300 rooms
- **Phaistos** — Southern Crete; site of the mysterious **Phaistos Disc**, a unique stamped clay disc with undeciphered symbols
- **Malia** — Eastern Crete; notable for the gold "bee pendant"
- **Zakros** — Easternmost palace; discovered largely undisturbed by looters<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

## Art & Culture

<Cited status="yellow">
Minoan art is distinctive for its naturalism, vivid colors, and themes: **bull-leaping** frescoes, marine motifs (octopuses, dolphins), goddesses with snakes, and elaborate ritual scenes. The absence of fortification walls at Minoan palaces has led (controversially) to characterizations of the Minoans as a peaceful, maritime civilization — the "Pax Minoica."<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

## Writing Systems

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Cretan Hieroglyphic** — The earliest writing on Crete (c. 2100–1700 BCE), still undeciphered
- **Linear A** — The main Minoan script (c. 1800–1450 BCE), used for administrative records; undeciphered, and the underlying language is unknown (not Greek)
- **Linear B** — Adopted by Mycenaean Greeks after they took control of Knossos (~1450 BCE); deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952 as an early form of Greek
</Cited>

## The Thera Eruption: Dating Controversy

<Cited status="green">
The great eruption of Thera (Santorini) is one of the most disputed dates in Bronze Age chronology. Radiocarbon dates on an olive branch sealed by Late Bronze Age tephra at Akrotiri cluster c. 1627–1600 BCE,<Ref id={6} /> but cross-Mediterranean ceramic synchronisms with the Egyptian historical chronology argue for a low date c. 1530 BCE — the 'high vs. low chronology' debate has run for more than three decades.<Ref id={7} /> A 2020 reassessment of cosmogenic and dendrochronological data still leaves the question open.<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

## Linear A: What We Can and Cannot Read

<Cited status="green">
Linear A is **undeciphered**, but it is *partially readable*: roughly 70 of its ~90 syllabic signs are shared with the later, deciphered Linear B — we can therefore vocalise many words even though we cannot translate them.<Ref id={9} /> The Haghia Triada accounting tablets, edited in the GORILA corpus, preserve numerical, fractional, and commodity logograms whose semantic ranges are clear (oil, wine, sheep, men/women), even though the underlying Minoan language remains unknown.<Ref id={10} />
</Cited>

## Religion

<Cited status="yellow">
Minoan religion featured prominent female deities (snake goddesses, mountain mothers), bull symbolism, peak sanctuaries, and cave sanctuaries (Diktaian Cave, Kamares Cave). The "sacred horns" (*horns of consecration*) and double axe (*labrys*) were ubiquitous religious symbols. The labyrinth of myth may echo the complex palatial architecture.
</Cited>

## Collapse

<Cited status="yellow">
The catastrophic eruption of **Thera** (Santorini, c. 1628 or 1530 BCE — dating debated) devastated Minoan settlements. By ~1450 BCE, most Cretan palaces were destroyed, and Mycenaean Greeks took control of Knossos. The Minoan civilization was absorbed into the Mycenaean world.
</Cited>

## A Linear A Libation Formula (primary source)

  provenance="Standard Linear A libation formula, attested on the Apodoulou, Ayios Georgios, and Knossos (KN Za 10) tables, c. 1700–1450 BCE; Heraklion Archaeological Museum and others; corpus in Godart & Olivier, GORILA (1976–1985)"
  refId={11}
/>

<Cited status="green">
Linear A remains the most important undeciphered script of the Aegean Bronze Age, with about 1,500 known inscriptions on clay tablets and stone or ceramic offering vessels. The recurrence of the same fixed sequences — most famously the so-called 'libation formula' on stone tables — across distant sites strongly suggests a shared religious or administrative idiom, but in the absence of a bilingual we cannot yet assign meanings to most signs. Sound-values can be tentatively recovered by analogy with Linear B, which Mycenaean scribes adapted from Linear A around 1450 BCE.<Ref id={11} />
</Cited>
