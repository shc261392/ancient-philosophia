## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Phoenician is a Canaanite language closely related to Hebrew, spoken in the coastal city-states of **Tyre**, **Sidon**, **Byblos**, and **Beirut** (modern Lebanon). The Phoenicians' greatest legacy is their **alphabet** — the first widely-adopted consonantal writing system (abjad), which was transmitted to the Greeks (who added vowels), and thence to the Romans, giving us the Latin alphabet used worldwide today. Phoenician colonists carried their language across the Mediterranean; its late form, **Punic**, was the language of Carthage.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Punic: The Western Phoenician Diaspora

<Cited status="green">
After the western Phoenician colonies declared independence from the Levantine homeland in the 6th century BCE, the dialect spoken from Carthage to the Iberian peninsula diverged into what scholars call **Punic** (and later **Neo-Punic** after Carthage's destruction in 146 BCE). Punic preserved Phoenician roughly the way medieval Latin preserved Classical Latin in early Christian Europe — frozen as a prestige language while spoken vernaculars drifted.<Ref id={7} /> Augustine of Hippo (4th–5th century CE) still records Punic being spoken in the North African countryside more than five hundred years after Carthage fell, and Neo-Punic inscriptions appear in Latin alphabet transliteration in the early Imperial period.<Ref id={8} />
</Cited>

## Key Inscriptions

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Ahiram Sarcophagus** (c. 1000 BCE) — Byblos; one of the earliest alphabetic texts
- **KAI 26 (Karatepe Bilingual)** — Phoenician-Luwian bilingual from SE Turkey (8th century BCE)
- **Punic inscriptions from Carthage** — Thousands of votive stelae
- **Nora Stone** — One of the earliest Phoenician inscriptions in the western Mediterranean (Sardinia)
</Cited>
