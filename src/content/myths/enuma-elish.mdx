## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The **Enūma Eliš** ("When on High"), named after its opening words, is the Babylonian creation epic — a poem of approximately 1,100 lines on seven tablets that narrates the creation of the cosmos, the rise of the god **Marduk** to supreme kingship, and the creation of humanity. It is simultaneously a cosmogony, a theogony, and a work of political theology: the epic justifies Babylon's supremacy among cities by establishing Marduk's supremacy among gods, as Wilfred Lambert's definitive critical edition demonstrates.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
The poem was recited during the **Akītu** (New Year) festival at Babylon, the most important religious celebration of the Babylonian calendar. It was not merely a myth — it was a performed act of cosmic renewal, re-establishing order at the turn of each year.<Ref id={6} />
</Cited>

## The Narrative

### Tablets I–II: Primordial Beginnings
<Cited status="yellow">
Before heaven and earth existed, there were two primordial waters: **Apsu** (fresh water, male) and **Tiamat** (salt water, female). From their mingling, the first gods were generated. But the younger gods were noisy and disruptive. Apsu, unable to rest, conspired to destroy them. **Ea** (Enki) learned of the plot, put Apsu to sleep with a spell, and killed him. Ea then established his dwelling upon Apsu's body and there begat **Marduk** — the greatest of the gods.<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
Tiamat, urged on by her vizier **Kingu**, raised an army of monsters to avenge Apsu: vipers, dragons, the *lahmu*, the *kusarikku* (bison-man), the *uğallu* (lion-demon), the scorpion-man, and other terrible beings (Tablet II.13–30 in Lambert's edition). She conferred supreme authority on Kingu by giving him the **Tablet of Destinies** — the cuneiform document that fixes the cosmic order.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

### Tablets III–IV: The Rise of Marduk
<Cited status="yellow">
The elder gods — Ea, then Anu — prove unable to face Tiamat. The young god Marduk volunteers, but demands a price: if he defeats Tiamat, he must be granted supreme authority over all gods. The divine assembly agrees. Armed with the winds, a net, a mace, and a bow, Marduk confronts Tiamat.<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
The battle on Tablet IV is the climax of the epic: Marduk traps Tiamat in his net, drives the *imhullu* ("evil wind") and the four winds into her mouth so that she cannot close it, and pierces her distended belly with an arrow that splits her heart. He captures Kingu and seizes the Tablet of Destinies, then re-binds it on his own breast as a sign of his new sovereignty.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

### Tablets IV–V: Creation from Chaos
<Cited status="yellow">
Marduk splits Tiamat's body in two:
- One half becomes **heaven** (the sky)
- The other half becomes **earth**<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
From Tiamat's eyes flow the Tigris and Euphrates. Marduk then organises the cosmos: he sets the constellations in their courses, establishes the lunar months and the calendar, and assigns stations to the great gods (Tablet V.1–46 in Talon's critical edition). The universe in the Babylonian imagination is literally Tiamat's transformed body — chaos made into ordered space.<Ref id={4} />
</Cited>

### Tablet VI: The Creation of Humanity
<Cited status="yellow">
Kingu is executed, and from his blood Ea creates **humanity** — *lullû*, "the savage" — whose purpose is to do the labour of the gods, freeing the divine beings from toil (Enūma Eliš VI.5–40 in Lambert's edition).<Ref id={1} /> This is the Mesopotamian answer to the question "Why were humans created?" — not out of love, but out of divine convenience, a theme paralleled in the *Atrahasis* epic.<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

### Tablet VII: The Fifty Names
<Cited status="yellow">
The final tablet consists of a litany of Marduk's fifty names, each encoding an aspect of his power and cosmic function. This theological catalog affirms Marduk's all-encompassing supremacy.
</Cited>

## Political Theology

<Cited status="yellow">
The Enūma Eliš is inseparable from the political history of Babylon. The poem elevates Marduk — originally a minor city god — to cosmic kingship, mirroring and legitimating Babylon's rise to political dominance under the First Dynasty of Babylon (Hammurabi's era) and especially the later Kassite and Neo-Babylonian periods.
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
In Assyria, the poem was adapted with **Ashur** replacing Marduk — the same theological structure serving a different imperial power. This demonstrates how Mesopotamian theology was a flexible tool of political legitimation. See also: [Mesopotamian Pantheon](/myths/mesopotamian-pantheon/).
</Cited>

## The Akītu Festival

<Cited status="yellow">
During the eleven-day Akītu (New Year) festival, the Enūma Eliš was recited in full before the statue of Marduk in the Esagila temple. The king played a ritual role — his authority was symbolically stripped and restored, mirroring the cosmic drama of order threatened and reestablished. The festival was a reenactment of creation: each new year began with the defeat of chaos.
</Cited>

## Comparative Significance

<Cited status="yellow">
The Enūma Eliš participates in a broad Near Eastern pattern of combat myths:
</Cited>

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Baal vs. Yam** (Ugaritic) — Storm god defeats the sea
- **Zeus vs. Typhon** (Greek) — Sky god defeats chaos monster
- **Teshub vs. Ullikummi** (Hittite-Hurrian) — Storm god defeats stone monster
- **Genesis 1** — While very different in theology, shares the motif of creation from watery chaos, the ordering of the cosmos, and the division of waters
</Cited>

## Primary Sources

- **Enūma Eliš tablets** — Seven major tablets; best-preserved copies from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and from Babylon
- **Ritual texts from Babylon** — Describing the Akītu festival and the recitation of the poem
- **Assyrian adaptations** — Substituting Ashur for Marduk
