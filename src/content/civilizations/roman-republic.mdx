## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The Roman Republic (509–27 BCE) was one of history's most consequential political experiments. From a small city-state on the Tiber, Rome expanded through a combination of military prowess, adaptable alliances, strategic road-building, and a flexible constitution to dominate the entire Mediterranean world. Its internal tensions — between patricians and plebeians, between Senate and popular assemblies, between Republican tradition and military strongmen — ultimately produced the civil wars that ended the Republic.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Institutions

<Cited status="yellow">
The Republican constitution was unwritten and evolved over centuries:
- **Consuls** — Two annually elected chief magistrates with military command (*imperium*)
- **Senate** — Advisory body of ex-magistrates; dominated policy through *auctoritas*
- **Tribunes of the Plebs** — Protected plebeian rights; could veto any magistrate's action
- **Assemblies** — The *comitia centuriata* (military assembly) and *concilium plebis* (plebeian assembly) passed laws and elected officials
- **Twelve Tables** (c. 450 BCE) — Rome's first written law code<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## The Conflict of the Orders (494–287 BCE)

<Cited status="yellow">
The long struggle between patricians (hereditary aristocracy) and plebeians (common citizens) resulted in the progressive opening of political offices to plebeians, the creation of the tribunate, and the establishment of written law.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## The Twelve Tables (primary source)

  originalLang="la"
  translation={`If a man breaks another's limb, and does not come to an agreement with him, let there be retaliation in kind.`}
  provenance="Twelve Tables (Lex Duodecim Tabularum) VIII.2, c. 451–450 BCE; preserved in quotation by Festus, De verborum significatione p. 363 L; ed. Crawford, Roman Statutes II (London, 1996), pp. 555–721"
  refId={11}
/>

<Cited status="green">
The **Twelve Tables** were Rome's first written law-code, the product of a decade-long political struggle in which the plebs demanded that customary law be codified and made public. Although the original bronze tablets displayed in the Forum perished, perhaps in the Gallic sack of 390 BCE, hundreds of fragments survive as quotations in later Roman jurists, antiquarians, and grammarians; Michael Crawford's 1996 *Roman Statutes* II provides the definitive modern reconstruction with text, translation, and commentary. The provisions of Table VIII illustrate the archaic *lex talionis* — still negotiable for cash compensation — that would later be displaced by classical Roman private law.<Ref id={11} />
</Cited>

## Expansion & the Punic Wars

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Italian conquest** (5th–3rd c. BCE) — Latin War, Samnite Wars, war with Pyrrhus of Epirus
- **First Punic War** (264–241 BCE) — Rome defeated Carthage and gained Sicily
- **Second Punic War** (218–201 BCE) — **Hannibal** crossed the Alps and devastated Italy for 15 years. **Scipio Africanus** defeated him at **Zama** (202 BCE)
- **Third Punic War** (149–146 BCE) — Carthage destroyed; Africa became a Roman province
- **Eastern conquests** — Macedonia (168 BCE), Corinth destroyed (146 BCE), Pergamon bequeathed (133 BCE)
</Cited>

## Polybius and the ‘Mixed Constitution’

<Cited status="green">
The Greek hostage **Polybius** (c. 200–118 BCE), brought to Rome after Pydna, gave the most influential ancient analysis of Republican success. In Book VI of his *Histories* he describes the Roman constitution as a balanced *politeia* combining monarchic (consuls), aristocratic (Senate), and democratic (assemblies) elements — a model that shaped Cicero's *De Re Publica*, Machiavelli's *Discorsi*, and Montesquieu's *Spirit of the Laws*, and that lies behind the separation of powers in the U.S. Constitution.<Ref id={6} /> Walbank's three-volume historical commentary remains the standard scholarly companion to Polybius.<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

## The Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE)

<Cited status="green">
The **Twelve Tables** were Rome's first written law code, fixed on bronze plaques in the Forum after the plebeian secession of 451–450 BCE. Although the bronze originals were lost in the Gallic sack of 390 BCE, schoolboys still recited the laws in Cicero's youth (*De Legibus* 2.59), and Crawford's *Roman Statutes* reconstructs the surviving fragments.<Ref id={8} /> The code regulates summons to court, debt-bondage, parental authority, theft, and burial — a snapshot of an archaic, agrarian society in transition to a complex civic state.<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

## The Late Republic Crisis

<Cited status="yellow">
The conquest of a Mediterranean empire strained Republican institutions beyond breaking. Erich Gruen's *Last Generation of the Roman Republic* and Harriet Flower's *Roman Republics* offer competing modern frameworks for the period:<Ref id={10} />
- **The Gracchi** (133–121 BCE) — Tiberius and Gaius attempted land reform; both were murdered<Ref id={1} />
- **Marius** — Military reforms (professional army loyal to commanders, not the state)<Ref id={1} />
- **Sulla** — First march on Rome, dictatorship, proscriptions<Ref id={1} />
- **First Triumvirate** — Caesar, Pompey, Crassus<Ref id={1} />
- **Caesar's Gallic Wars** (58–50 BCE) — Conquered Gaul, crossed the Rubicon (49 BCE)<Ref id={3} />
- **Caesar's assassination** (March 15, 44 BCE) — By Brutus, Cassius, and other senators<Ref id={1} />
- **Second Triumvirate** — Octavian, Antony, Lepidus; Actium (31 BCE); **Octavian becomes Augustus** (27 BCE)<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>
