## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Alexander III of Macedon (356–323 BCE) conquered the entire Achaemenid Persian Empire in little more than a decade, creating the largest empire yet seen and inaugurating the **Hellenistic Age** — a period of Greek cultural diffusion across the Near East, Egypt, and Central Asia. After his death, his generals (the *Diadochi*) carved up the empire into rival kingdoms that endured until Rome absorbed them one by one.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Alexander's Conquests

<Cited status="green">
Tutored by Aristotle and inheriting Philip II's superb Macedonian army, Alexander launched his invasion of Persia in 334 BCE:
- **Granicus** (334 BCE) — First victory, opening Anatolia
- **Issus** (333 BCE) — Defeated Darius III in person
- Conquest of **Egypt** — Founded **Alexandria**, consulted the oracle of Amun at Siwa
- **Gaugamela** (331 BCE) — Decisive defeat of the Persian army, collapse of the Achaemenid Empire
- Campaigns into **Central Asia** and the **Indus Valley**
- Death in Babylon, June 323 BCE, aged 32<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## The Successor Kingdoms (Diadochi)

<Cited status="yellow">
The Wars of the Diadochi (322–281 BCE) produced three major kingdoms:
- **Ptolemaic Egypt** (305–30 BCE) — Founded by Ptolemy I. Built the **Library and Museum of Alexandria**, made Egypt the intellectual center of the Mediterranean. The **Rosetta Stone** (196 BCE) and Cleopatra VII (the last Ptolemaic ruler) are its most famous legacies
- **Seleucid Empire** (312–63 BCE) — The largest successor state, spanning from Syria to Iran. Promoted Hellenization but ruled diverse populations
- **Antigonid Macedonia** (306–168 BCE) — Controlled Greece and Macedonia
- **Attalid Pergamon** (282–133 BCE) — Brilliant cultural center in western Anatolia, famous for the Great Altar of Pergamon<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Hellenistic Culture

<Cited status="green">
**Koine Greek** became the common language from Italy to Afghanistan — the language of the Septuagint, the New Testament, and the administrative archives of Ptolemaic Egypt. The period saw extraordinary achievements documented across Peter Green's *Alexander to Actium* and the *Cambridge Companion to the Hellenistic World*:<Ref id={6} />
- **Science** — Euclid's *Elements* fixed Greek geometry; Archimedes of Syracuse computed π between 223/71 and 22/7; Eratosthenes of Cyrene measured the Earth's circumference to within a few percent of the modern value; Aristarchus of Samos proposed a heliocentric model (Archimedes, *Sand Reckoner* 1.4–7).<Ref id={7} />
- **Philosophy** — Stoicism (Zeno of Citium), Epicureanism (Epicurus), Pyrrhonian Skepticism — schools synthesised in Long & Sedley's *The Hellenistic Philosophers* (Cambridge, 1987).<Ref id={8} />
- **Art** — The Laocoön group (Vatican Museums), the Nike of Samothrace (Louvre Ma 2369), and the Pergamon Altar (Berlin) embody the period's emotional intensity and dramatic spatial composition.<Ref id={2} />
- **Religion** — The cult of Serapis, devised by Ptolemy I to unite Greek and Egyptian populations, and the diffusion of Isis worship around the Mediterranean.<Ref id={6} />
</Cited>

## The Library of Alexandria

<Cited status="green">
Founded by Ptolemy I and expanded by Ptolemy II, the Mouseion and Library of Alexandria functioned as a state-funded research institute. Roger Bagnall's reassessment of the ancient testimonia argues that the often-quoted figure of 'half a million scrolls' is rhetorical rather than literal, but the institution's importance as the editorial centre for Homer, the tragedians, and the early scientific corpus is beyond dispute.<Ref id={9} /> The Library's decline was gradual: it was damaged by fire in Caesar's Alexandrian War (48 BCE), but a daughter library at the Serapeum survived until the late 4th century CE.<Ref id={10} />
</Cited>

## Legacy

<Cited status="yellow">
The Hellenistic world was gradually absorbed by Rome (Corinth destroyed 146 BCE, Ptolemaic Egypt annexed 30 BCE), but Greek language and culture remained dominant in the eastern Mediterranean throughout the Roman period and into the Byzantine era.
</Cited>
