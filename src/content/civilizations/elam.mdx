## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Elam was one of the oldest civilizations of the ancient Near East, located in southwestern Iran (modern Khuzestan and Fars provinces). Centered on the great cities of **Susa** and **Anshan**, Elam maintained a complex and often antagonistic relationship with Mesopotamia over three millennia. It developed its own writing systems and a distinctive culture that blended Mesopotamian and indigenous Iranian elements.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Writing Systems

<Cited status="green">
Elam produced two indigenous scripts: **Proto-Elamite** (c. 3100–2900 BCE), which remains largely undeciphered, and **Linear Elamite** (c. 2300–1900 BCE), which has seen significant recent progress in decipherment (Desset 2022). Elamite scribes also used Mesopotamian cuneiform to write the Elamite language — itself a language isolate with no known relatives.<Ref id={4} />
</Cited>

## Historical Periods

<Cited status="yellow">
- **Proto-Elamite** (c. 3200–2700 BCE) — Early state formation, Proto-Elamite script spread to sites across the Iranian plateau
- **Old Elamite** (c. 2700–1500 BCE) — Awan and Shimashki dynasties; frequent conflicts with Mesopotamian powers
- **Middle Elamite** (c. 1500–1100 BCE) — The apex of Elamite power. King **Untash-Napirisha** built the magnificent ziggurat at Chogha Zanbil (UNESCO World Heritage Site). King **Shutruk-Nahhunte** raided Babylon and carried off the Code of Hammurabi stele and the Victory Stele of Naram-Sin to Susa (where they were later found by French archaeologists)
- **Neo-Elamite** (c. 1100–539 BCE) — Decline and conflict with Assyria; Ashurbanipal sacked Susa in 646 BCE. Elamite culture persisted and influenced the Achaemenid Persians, who used Elamite as one of their administrative languages
</Cited>

## Legacy

<Cited status="yellow">
Elam's influence on the Achaemenid Persian Empire was profound — Susa became a Persian capital, and Elamite was one of the three official languages inscribed at Persepolis and Behistun.
</Cited>

## The 2022 Linear Elamite Decipherment

<Cited status="green">
For more than a century **Linear Elamite** — a syllabary attested on roughly forty inscribed silver vessels and stone objects of the late third millennium BCE — resisted decipherment. In 2022, François Desset and collaborators published a systematic reading in the *Zeitschrift für Assyriologie*, using a corpus of bilingual royal inscriptions of King Puzur-Inšušinak of Awan (c. 2100 BCE) as their key: the Akkadian column supplies the royal names and titles, the Linear Elamite column supplies the phonetic values.<Ref id={5} /> The result is roughly 95% of signs read, vindicating an indigenous Iranian writing tradition independent of Mesopotamian cuneiform.<Ref id={6} />
</Cited>

## Chogha Zanbil and the Royal City of Untash-Napirisha

<Cited status="green">
The Middle Elamite king **Untash-Napirisha** (c. 1340–1300 BCE) founded a new ritual capital at *Dūr-Untaš* (modern **Chogha Zanbil**) west of Susa. Roman Ghirshman's 1951–1962 excavations exposed a 52-metre-tall ziggurat — the largest surviving outside Mesopotamia — at the centre of a sacred precinct of eleven temples, surrounded by three concentric enclosure walls.<Ref id={7} /> The site, abandoned shortly after the king's death and never reoccupied, is a uniquely preserved Bronze Age cult landscape; it became Iran's first UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>
