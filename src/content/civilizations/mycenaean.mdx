## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The Mycenaean civilization was the first civilization of mainland Greece and the first to speak a form of the Greek language. Named after the citadel of **Mycenae** in the Argolid, it flourished during the Late Bronze Age and was part of the international network that included Egypt, the Hittites, and Babylon. The Mycenaeans are the likely historical basis for Homer's Achaeans.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Key Sites & Architecture

<Cited status="green">
Mycenaean civilization was characterized by powerful fortified citadels with **cyclopean walls** (massive stone blocks attributed by later Greeks to the Cyclopes):
- **Mycenae** — The **Lion Gate**, **Treasury of Atreus** (tholos tomb), Grave Circles A and B (with the gold "Mask of Agamemnon")
- **Tiryns** — Massive fortifications, elaborate palatial complex
- **Pylos** — Palace of Nestor, source of most Linear B tablets
- **Thebes** — Major center with its own Linear B archive
- **Athens** — Cyclopean walls on the Acropolis<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>

## Linear B

<Cited status="green">
**Linear B** was deciphered by **Michael Ventris** in 1952, with assistance from John Chadwick — one of the great intellectual achievements of the 20th century. The tablets, written in an early form of Greek, are primarily administrative records documenting palace inventories, landholdings, personnel, and religious offerings. They reveal a complex bureaucratic society governed by a **wanax** (king) and **lawagetas** (military leader).<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Society & Warfare

<Cited status="yellow">
Mycenaean society was hierarchical and militaristic. Elite warriors used bronze armor, figure-eight shields, and war chariots. The **Uluburun shipwreck** (c. 1300 BCE, off Turkey) reveals the scale of Late Bronze Age maritime trade — carrying goods from at least seven cultures. Hittite texts reference **Ahhiyawa**, likely the Mycenaean Greeks, as a major Anatolian coastal power.
</Cited>

## Religion

<Cited status="yellow">
Linear B tablets mention many deities familiar from later Greek religion: Zeus (di-wo), Hera (e-ra), Poseidon (po-se-da-o), Artemis (a-te-mi-to), Dionysus (di-wo-nu-so), and Athena (a-ta-na-po-ti-ni-ja). This demonstrates remarkable continuity between Bronze Age and Classical Greek religion.
</Cited>

## Bronze Age Collapse (c. 1200–1100 BCE)

<Cited status="yellow">
Around 1200 BCE, every major Mycenaean palace was destroyed or abandoned within approximately a generation. The causes remain debated: earthquake, drought, internal conflict, systems collapse, or invasion. Greece entered a "Dark Age" lasting several centuries, during which writing was lost and population plummeted.
</Cited>

## Ahhiyawa and the Hittite Evidence for the Mycenaeans

<Cited status="green">
For much of the 20th century the identification of Hittite **Ahhiyawa** with Homer's *Achaioi* (Achaeans / Mycenaean Greeks) was contested. Beckman, Bryce, and Cline's 2011 SBL edition collects all 26 surviving Hittite documents (KUB and KBo numbers) that mention Ahhiyawa across two and a half centuries of Hittite imperial correspondence — including the famous **Tawagalawa Letter** (KUB 14.3) in which the Hittite king addresses the king of Ahhiyawa as 'My Brother' and 'Great King', the Hittite formula reserved for equals such as the pharaoh.<Ref id={6} /> The case for the identification (and for Miletus = Hittite Millawanda as a Mycenaean enclave on the Anatolian coast) is now the consensus position.<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

## The Pylos Combat Agate (2017) and Mycenaean Art

<Cited status="green">
The University of Cincinnati team excavating the unplundered shaft grave of the **"Griffin Warrior"** at Pylos (c. 1450 BCE) recovered in 2015 a tiny carnelian sealstone (3.6 cm) showing a Mycenaean warrior driving a sword into the throat of an opponent; the level of anatomical realism — visible facial features, musculature, drapery — is closer to Classical Greek art than to anything previously known from the Aegean Bronze Age.<Ref id={8} /> The Stocker–Davis publication argues that the gem, alongside the four gold signet rings from the same grave, demands a substantial revision of how we date the emergence of figural narrative in Aegean art.<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>
