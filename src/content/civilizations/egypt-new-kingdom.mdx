## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The New Kingdom (Dynasties 18–20) was ancient Egypt's imperial age and its most thoroughly documented period. After expelling the Hyksos, Egypt transformed into a military superpower controlling an empire stretching from Nubia to the Euphrates. This era produced many of Egypt's most famous rulers and monuments, and saw Egypt engage as a major player in the international "Club of Great Powers" alongside the Hittites, Mitanni, Babylon, and Assyria.<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>

## 18th Dynasty: Egypt's Golden Age

<Cited status="green">
- **Ahmose I** — Expelled the Hyksos and founded the New Kingdom
- **Hatshepsut** — One of the rare female pharaohs, built the stunning mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari, sent a famous trading expedition to Punt
- **Thutmose III** — "The Napoleon of Egypt," conducted 17 campaigns into the Levant, expanded the empire to its greatest extent
- **Amenhotep III** — Presided over Egypt at its wealthiest; magnificent building program, Amarna Letters diplomacy
- **Akhenaten** — Initiated a radical religious revolution promoting the sole worship of the Aten (sun-disc), founded a new capital at Amarna; his queen was Nefertiti
- **Tutankhamun** — The boy-king whose intact tomb, discovered by Howard Carter in 1922, captivated the world<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## The Merneptah Stele (primary source)

<Cited status="green">
The **Merneptah Stele** — a triumphal hymn celebrating Merneptah's Libyan campaign discovered by Flinders Petrie at Thebes in 1896 — contains the earliest secure extra-biblical reference to **Israel**. The name is written with the determinative for *people* rather than *land*, suggesting that c. 1208 BCE Israel was understood by Egyptian scribes as a non-urbanised population group in Canaan rather than a settled kingdom. The text thus provides a *terminus ante quem* for the existence of an entity called Israel in the southern Levant.<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

## Ramesside Period (19th–20th Dynasties)

<Cited status="green">
- **Ramesses II** (r. c. 1279–1213 BCE) — Egypt's longest-reigning pharaoh, fought the Battle of Kadesh against the Hittites (c. 1274 BCE) and concluded the world's earliest known peace treaty. Built Abu Simbel, the Ramesseum, and much of Karnak and Luxor temples
- **Merneptah** — His victory stele contains the earliest known reference to "Israel"
- **Ramesses III** — Defended Egypt against the mysterious **Sea Peoples** confederation (~1177 BCE)<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Religion & Funerary Culture

<Cited status="yellow">
The Valley of the Kings (across from Thebes/Luxor) became the royal necropolis. The **Book of the Dead** guided the deceased through the underworld. The great temples of Karnak and Luxor were continually expanded. Amun-Ra became Egypt's supreme deity, and the Amun priesthood at Thebes accumulated enormous power and wealth.
</Cited>

## Collapse

<Cited status="yellow">
The New Kingdom ended with the Third Intermediate Period — internal power struggles, the division of Egypt between pharaohs in Tanis and high priests in Thebes, and the loss of the Levantine empire.
</Cited>

## The Amarna Letters and Late Bronze Age Diplomacy

<Cited status="green">
Discovered c. 1887 in the ruins of Akhetaten (Tell el-Amarna), the **Amarna Letters** are roughly 380 clay tablets — most written in Middle Babylonian Akkadian, the diplomatic lingua franca — exchanged between the Egyptian court of Amenhotep III and Akhenaten and the rulers of Babylonia, Mitanni, Hatti, Assyria, Cyprus (Alashiya), and the Canaanite city-states.<Ref id={6} /> They reveal a Late Bronze Age "Club of Great Powers" operating on a system of dynastic marriage, gift exchange (*šulmānu*), and formal address as 'brother' (*aḫu*) — and the desperate appeals of Canaanite vassals like Rib-Hadda of Byblos asking Pharaoh for archers against the encroaching Habiru.<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

## Tutankhamun's Tomb and the Carter Archive

<Cited status="green">
The **discovery of KV62** by Howard Carter on 4 November 1922 yielded the only nearly-intact royal burial of the New Kingdom — over 5,000 objects, the celebrated gold mask, the three nested coffins, and the canopic shrine. Carter's ten-year excavation diary, his card index, and Harry Burton's 1,400 glass-plate photographs were digitised by the **Griffith Institute (Oxford)** and made freely available online; they remain the primary source for everything written on the tomb.<Ref id={8} /> Recent re-analyses using DNA, CT scanning, and chemical residue (Hawass 2010, Forbes 2018) have illuminated Tutankhamun's parentage, his probable death from a combination of malaria and a leg fracture, and the rapid sealing of the tomb.<Ref id={4} />
</Cited>
