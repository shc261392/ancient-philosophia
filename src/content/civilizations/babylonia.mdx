## Overview

<Cited status="green">
Babylonia was the civilization centered on the great city of Babylon, located on the Euphrates in central Mesopotamia (modern Iraq, south of Baghdad). It produced some of antiquity's most significant achievements in law, mathematics, astronomy, and literature. Babylon's name became synonymous with urban splendor and learning throughout the ancient world.<Ref id={2} />
</Cited>

## Old Babylonian Period (c. 1894–1595 BCE)

<Cited status="green">
The First Dynasty of Babylon was established by Amorite rulers. Its greatest king, **Hammurabi** (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE), united most of Mesopotamia and promulgated his famous **Law Code** — one of the oldest and most complete legal codes, inscribed on a basalt stele now in the Louvre. This period also saw the composition of major literary works, including the creation epic *Enuma Elish* and important versions of the Gilgamesh epic. The Old Babylonian dialect became the classical form of the Akkadian language.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Kassite & Middle Babylonian Period (c. 1595–1155 BCE)

<Cited status="green">
After the Hittite sack of Babylon (~1595 BCE), the Kassite dynasty ruled for over 400 years — one of the longest dynasties in Mesopotamian history. This era saw Babylon emerge as a major international power, corresponding with Egypt as an equal (Amarna Letters). The Kassite period also saw the standardization of the Babylonian literary tradition.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Neo-Babylonian Empire (626–539 BCE)

<Cited status="yellow">
The Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) Empire was Babylon's final period of independence and glory:
- **Nabopolassar** (r. 626–605 BCE) — Established the dynasty and helped destroy the Assyrian Empire
- **Nebuchadnezzar II** (r. 605–562 BCE) — Rebuilt Babylon magnificently, constructing the Ishtar Gate, the Processional Way, and the legendary Hanging Gardens (one of the Seven Wonders). He also conquered Jerusalem and deported the Judean elite (the Babylonian Exile of 586 BCE)
- The empire fell to **Cyrus the Great** of Persia in 539 BCE
</Cited>

<Cited status="green">
The **Code of Hammurabi** is the most complete surviving Mesopotamian law collection — 282 case-law paragraphs framed by a hymnic prologue and curse-laden epilogue, cut into a 2.25 m basalt stele crowned with a relief of Hammurabi receiving the rod and ring of justice from Šamaš, the sun-god and divine judge. Like Narām-Sîn's stele, it was carried as war-booty from Sippar to Susa by Šutruk-Naḫḫunte of Elam in the 12th century BCE; it was excavated by Jacques de Morgan in 1901 and has been on permanent display in the Louvre ever since.<Ref id={9} />
</Cited>

## Astronomy & Mathematics

<Cited status="yellow">
Babylonian scholars made remarkable advances in both fields. They developed a sexagesimal (base-60) number system — the origin of our 60-minute hours and 360-degree circles. Late Babylonian astronomers produced highly accurate ephemerides and were the first to use mathematical models to predict celestial phenomena.
</Cited>

## Hammurabi's Code: Composition, Discovery, and Influence

<Cited status="green">
The basalt stele inscribed with the **Laws of Hammurabi** was looted from Sippar to Susa by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte in the 12th century BCE; Jacques de Morgan's French expedition uncovered it there in 1901 and it has stood in the Louvre ever since.<Ref id={6} /> Its 282 case-law paragraphs — preceded by a long prologue glorifying the king as 'shepherd of the people' and followed by an epilogue invoking curses on anyone who tampers with the text — are not so much a comprehensive law code as a royal monument advertising Hammurabi as the just king who 'has caused justice to shine forth in the land'.<Ref id={1} /> Martha Roth's SBL edition is the standard English translation; Marc Van De Mieroop's biography sets the laws in their broader Old Babylonian context.<Ref id={7} />
</Cited>

## Babylonian Mathematical Astronomy and the ACT Procedure Texts

<Cited status="green">
Late Babylonian astronomers of the Achaemenid and Seleucid periods (c. 500–50 BCE) achieved something extraordinary: the use of **mathematical models** — column-based linear and zigzag functions — to *predict* lunar and planetary phenomena rather than merely recording them. Otto Neugebauer's *Astronomical Cuneiform Texts* (1955) catalogued the corpus; Mathieu Ossendrijver's 2012 monograph showed that these procedure texts include numerical integration of velocity to compute Jupiter's position — a method previously thought to originate with Oxford Calculators in the 14th century CE.<Ref id={8} /> The two-day arc-segment text TU 11 (BM 36761) anticipates the concept of definite integration by more than 1,400 years.<Ref id={5} />
</Cited>
