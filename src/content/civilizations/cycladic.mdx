## Overview

<Cited status="green">
The Cycladic civilization flourished on the Cyclades — a group of islands in the central Aegean Sea including Naxos, Paros, Syros, Keros, and Thera (Santorini). While less politically complex than the later Minoans or Mycenaeans, the Cycladic culture produced some of the most haunting and aesthetically powerful art of the ancient world — the iconic white marble figurines that inspired artists from Brâncuși to Modigliani and Henry Moore.<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

## Marble Figurines

<Cited status="yellow">
The **canonical folded-arm figurines** (c. 2800–2300 BCE) are the defining achievement of Cycladic art. These abstracted female forms, carved from the fine white marble of Naxos and Paros, range from a few centimeters to nearly life-size. Originally painted with details (eyes, hair, jewelry) in red, blue, and black pigments, they have survived in their stripped marble state — which paradoxically enhances their modern aesthetic appeal. Their purpose remains debated: funerary offerings, cult images, or representations of a deity.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>

## Material Culture

<Cited status="green">
Beyond figurines, the Cycladics produced:
- **Marble vessels** — Elegant bowls, cups, and palettes
- **Metalwork** — Bronze tools and weapons, silver jewelry
- **"Frying pans"** — Flat ceramic discs with incised decoration, possibly related to maritime symbolism<Ref id={3} />
</Cited>

## Settlements & Society

<Cited status="yellow">
Key sites include **Chalandriani** on Syros (a fortified settlement with over 600 graves), **Keros** (a major ritual deposit site where broken figurines were deliberately gathered), and **Akrotiri** on Thera (which later became a major Minoan colony). The Cycladic islanders were connected by maritime trade routes, exchanging obsidian from Melos and emery from Naxos across the Aegean.
</Cited>

## Legacy

<Cited status="yellow">
The Cycladic culture formed part of the broader Aegean Bronze Age world that would later give rise to Minoan and Mycenaean civilizations. The figurines remain the most recognized symbol of prehistoric Aegean art.
</Cited>

## Keros and the Special Deposit

<Cited status="green">
The tiny island of **Keros**, midway between Naxos and Amorgos, contains a unique site: the **Kavos Special Deposit**, where for roughly five centuries (c. 2750–2300 BCE) Cycladic islanders ritually deposited thousands of *intentionally broken* marble figurines and vessels brought from across the archipelago. Colin Renfrew's renewed excavations (2006–2008, 2016–2018) showed that almost every piece is a single fragment whose match has never been found — strong evidence that each was broken elsewhere and only one piece carried to Keros as part of a deliberate ritual.<Ref id={5} /> The adjacent islet of **Dhaskalio** preserves the largest Early Bronze Age settlement in the Aegean, with imported marble architecture and metallurgical workshops.<Ref id={6} />
</Cited>

## Provenancing the Marble

<Cited status="green">
Isotope and trace-element analysis of Cycladic marble (e.g. the Cambridge–Athens collaboration of Herz, Wenner, and Manfra) has shown that almost all canonical figurines were carved from **Naxian** or **Parian** white marble — the two islands with the finest fine-grained quarries — and that distribution radiates outward from these centres.<Ref id={7} /> Recent isotopic work also supports the view of Pat Getz-Gentle that distinct sculptural "hands" can be identified, with individual masters producing recognisable stylistic groups across decades — a degree of artistic individuation otherwise unparalleled in prehistoric Europe.<Ref id={1} />
</Cited>
